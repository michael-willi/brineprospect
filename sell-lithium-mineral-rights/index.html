Should I sell my lithium mineral rights? How to check an offer

An offer to buy your lithium rights is a price for a royalty that does not exist yet. Whether it is fair depends on what the buyer assumed about four things: the lithium concentration in your water, the lithium price, how long a plant takes and the chance one gets built. Developers test before they make an offer. Most owners have never seen a number for their own water. This page shows how to back the assumptions out of an offer, and what selling gives up compared with leasing.

Key facts

  • A sale: one payment now. The buyer takes the royalty and the upside for good.
  • A lease: a signing bonus now and a royalty if a plant is built. The rights stay yours.
  • What sets the price: concentration, lithium price, timing and the chance a plant is built.
  • Public benchmarks: one. Arkansas set a 2.5% lithium royalty in 2025. Sale prices and bonus terms are private.
  • Base-case value: about $164 to $2,100 per net mineral acre today over the East Texas Smackover, depending on concentration.

What is a buyer paying for?

Until a plant is producing, lithium rights pay nothing. What a buyer is paying for is the chance of a royalty later. The math is the same as in the lithium rights value calculator: estimate the royalty your acres or your water would earn each year, discount it for the years before the first check, and multiply by the chance a plant is ever built.

A buyer then pays less than that figure, because a buyer keeps a margin. So a fair offer sits somewhat under the estimated value today. How far under is a negotiation.

What does an offer assume about my brine?

Run the math backward and any offer turns into a lithium concentration. The table takes one net mineral acre over the East Texas Smackover on the calculator's base case: a 2.5% royalty, $15,000 a tonne, a 5-year wait, 20 years of payments and a 12% discount rate.

Offer per net mineral acreConcentration at which the estimate equals the offer
$500about 192 mg/L
$1,000about 383 mg/L
$1,500about 575 mg/L
$2,000about 766 mg/L

For reference, the median of public Smackover samples is 84 mg/L, the Franklin Project in East Texas averages 668 mg/L, and the highest reported there is 806 mg/L. An offer of $1,000 an acre matches the estimate at about 383 mg/L. If the brine under you runs at the Franklin average, the same acre comes to about $1,740 on the same assumptions. The offer is the same either way. The concentration decides whether it is fair.

The calculator does this for your own numbers. Enter your acres or your water, type the offer into the offer box, and it shows the concentration the offer assumes.

These are screening estimates built on judgment calls, including the odds that a plant is built. They are not an appraisal, and no figure here is an offer.

Selling compared with leasing

Sell the rightsLease the rights
What you get nowThe full price, onceA signing bonus
What you get laterNothingA royalty, if a plant is built and sells lithium
What you keepOnly what the deed does not conveyThe rights. The lease sets how long the developer can hold them.
If no plant is builtYou keep the priceYou keep the bonus and the rights
If the brine is richer than expectedThe buyer gainsYou gain through the royalty

Neither is right for everyone. A sale trades an uncertain royalty for certain money and hands the risk to someone else. A lease keeps you in if the plant works and leaves you with the bonus if it does not. Either one is a better decision with your own number in hand. The checklist for lithium leases covers what to read in a lease.

What should I ask before I sell?

  1. What exactly is being sold. Lithium only, all brine minerals, or every mineral under the tract. A mineral deed is permanent.
  2. Whether it is a sale or a lease. Some offers are leases with a bonus. The document decides, not the cover letter.
  3. What concentration the buyer is assuming. Ask for the number and where it came from. A buyer who has tested rarely shares the result, which is the reason to have your own.
  4. Whether the lithium is yours to sell. In Texas, lithium in produced water usually belongs to the operator, and ownership of deep brine is not written into statute. The guide covers who owns the lithium in each case.
  5. Who the buyer is. A developer putting acreage together for a plant, or a buyer who plans to lease or resell the rights.
  6. How long the offer stands. A short deadline leaves no time to test or to get a second opinion. Ask for more time.
  7. Whether you can keep a piece. A deed can reserve a share of the royalty to the seller. Ask whether the buyer will accept that.

A sale of mineral rights is permanent and has tax consequences. Have an oil and gas attorney read the deed, and ask a tax professional how the payment is treated, before you sign.

How do I get my own number?

The one input the calculator cannot know is the lithium in your water. A lab measures it from a sample, most often by ICP-OES, and reports it in milligrams per liter. If a well on or near your land produces brine or water from the formation, ask the operator for a sample.

Questions about selling lithium rights

Should I sell my lithium mineral rights?

It depends on what the offer assumes and on how much risk you want to carry. A sale pays once and ends your stake. A lease pays a signing bonus and keeps you in line for a royalty if a plant is built. Before choosing, work out the lithium concentration the offer assumes and compare it with what is known about the brine under your land. This is general information, not advice for your situation.

How much should I sell my lithium rights for?

Start from the estimated value today: the royalty your acres or your water could earn, discounted for the wait and multiplied by the chance a plant is built. On the calculator's base case that is about $164 per net mineral acre at 84 mg/L over the East Texas Smackover and about $2,100 at 806 mg/L. A buyer pays somewhat less than the estimate to keep a margin. Prices paid for lithium rights are rarely made public, so there is no market average to lean on.

Is an unsolicited offer a sign that my lithium rights are valuable?

It is a sign that someone has put a number on them. It does not tell you what the number is. Developers test before they make an offer, so the buyer often knows more about your water than you do. A buyer does not offer more than they think the rights are worth, so treat the offer as a floor on their estimate, not as the value.

Is it better to lease or sell lithium rights?

A lease keeps the rights yours and pays a royalty if a plant is built. A sale gives you certain money now and ends your stake. If the brine turns out richer than the buyer assumed, a lease pays you for it and a sale pays the buyer. If no plant is ever built, the seller comes out ahead.

Can I sell my lithium rights and keep my oil and gas rights?

Often yes, because lithium and brine can be conveyed as their own substance, but it depends on how your deeds and existing leases are written. Make sure the deed names only what you mean to sell, and have an attorney read the granting language before you sign.

Do I need to test my water before I sell lithium rights?

Nobody requires it. But concentration moves the value more than any other input, and it is the one the buyer is most likely to have and you are not. A screening result from your own sample tells you whether the offer deserves a counter. A developer will still resample before signing.
